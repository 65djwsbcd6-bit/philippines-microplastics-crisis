The Plastic & Microplastic Crisis in the Philippines

From a ten-peso sachet to a fish on your plate: how one of the world's most plastic-leaking archipelagos is filling its seas, soils, and bodies with particles it cannot take back.

Start with the basics

What counts as plastic pollution?

Plastics are grouped by size, and by how the particles came to be.

Macroplastics

Larger than 5 mm

Bottles, bags, wrappers, fishing nets. Visible, and the source of most entanglement and clogged waterways.

Microplastics

1 µm to 5 mm

Fragments, fibers, beads and pellets small enough to be eaten by plankton, shellfish and fish.

Nanoplastics

Smaller than 1 µm

Particles that may cross cell membranes. Hardest to detect, and the least understood in terms of health effects.

Primary vs. secondary microplastics

Manufactured small on purpose: microbeads in some cosmetics and scrubs, and the industrial pellets (nurdles) used to make plastic products. They enter water through drains and spills.

Formed when larger plastic breaks down through sunlight, waves and abrasion, or is shed by products such as synthetic clothing and car tires. Most microplastics in the environment are of this kind.

Status in the Philippines

An archipelago of more than 7,000 islands, heavy coastal populations and uneven waste collection makes the country one of the largest sources of plastic entering the ocean.

The sachet economy

Shampoo, coffee, detergent and sauces are sold in single-serve sachets that low-income households can afford daily. Multilayer sachets are cheap and convenient, but are rarely recyclable. Advocacy group GAIA has estimated that Filipinos use about 164 million sachets every day.

Rivers as conveyor belts

Studies of global river emissions place the Pasig River, which flows through Metro Manila into Manila Bay, among the largest river sources of ocean plastic. The Philippines hosts a large share of the world's most polluting rivers.

Causes

Single-use packaging

Sachets, thin bags and disposable cups are built into daily buying habits and into the sari-sari store retail model.

Fast fashion

Polyester, nylon and acrylic clothing sheds synthetic microfibers in every wash. Wastewater treatment is limited, so fibers flow to rivers and the sea.

Tire wear

Tires contain synthetic rubber and shed particles as they wear. Heavy traffic in cities like Metro Manila washes this dust into drains during rain.

Waste management gaps

Uneven collection, open dumps, few sorting and recycling facilities, and waste dumped along waterways let plastic escape before it is ever processed.

Effects

Sea turtles, seabirds and fish become entangled in nets and bags, or swallow plastic they mistake for food. Ingestion causes blocked guts, false fullness and starvation. Plastic also carries pathogens onto coral reefs.

Microplastics reach farmland through irrigation water, compost, sludge and degraded mulch film. They can alter soil structure and water retention and harm earthworms and soil microbes, affecting crop growth.

Plankton and filter feeders such as mussels take in particles, which move up to fish and then to people. Plastics also absorb toxic chemicals, which can build up (bioaccumulate) along the food chain.

People are exposed through seafood, salt, water and air. Plastics contain additives such as phthalates and bisphenols, which are known endocrine disruptors that interfere with hormones. Research on long-term effects is still developing.
