01. Scientific Framework & Classification

Plastic Pollution: Scale & Spectrum

Understanding marine plastic pollution requires examining how synthetic polymers degrade across physical sizes, chemical structures, and marine transport dynamics.

Size: > 25 mm

Macroplastics

Macroplastics are visible, large-scale synthetic waste items that enter marine pathways directly. In the Philippines, this category is dominated by discarded beverage bottles, single-use carrier bags, rigid containers, and abandoned fishing gear (ghost nets).

Primary Concern: Physical entanglement of wildlife and coastal drainage blockage leading to urban flooding.
Size: 5 mm – 25 mm

Mesoplastics

Mesoplastics represent intermediate plastic debris. These include bottle caps, broken plastic cutlery, drinking straws, and pre-production plastic pellets (nurdles) lost during maritime freight transport.

Primary Concern: High accumulation along high-tide coastal lines and shorelines across archipelago beaches.
Size: 1 µm – 5 mm

Secondary Microplastics

Secondary microplastics are created when macro-debris undergoes fragmentation in sunny tropical environments. Intense ultraviolet (UV) radiation breaks polymer bonds, while physical wave action grinds plastic pieces into microscopic particles.

Primary Concern: Readily ingested by filter feeders and small marine organisms mistaking them for food.
Size: Manufactured Micro-Scale

Primary Microplastics & Microfibers

Intentionally manufactured microscopic particles include exfoliants in cosmetics, industrial abrasives, and synthetic garment fibers (polyester, nylon). Washing synthetic clothes releases thousands of fibers per laundry cycle directly into waterways.

Primary Concern: Unfiltered bypass through standard municipal wastewater treatment systems.
Size: < 1 µm

Nanoplastics

Sub-micron scale particles formed through complete disintegration of synthetic polymers. Due to their minute dimensions, nanoplastics remain suspended in seawater as colloidal particles.

Primary Concern: Ability to cross cellular membranes, enter bloodstreams, and accumulate in biological organs.

Common Plastic Polymers Found in Philippine Waters

PET (Polyethylene Terephthalate)

Used in clear soda and water bottles. Dense, sinks in water, accumulating in estuarine sediments.

PE / PP (Polyethylene / Polypropylene)

Used in multi-layer sachets and plastic bags. Highly buoyant, drifting across sea surfaces.

PS (Polystyrene)

Used in takeaway food containers and styrofoam coolers. Fragmenting into buoyant micro-beads.

River Basin Focus

Pasig River Aquatic Transport

The Pasig River serves as a main aquatic conduit running through Metro Manila, transporting urban waste directly into Manila Bay. Environmental studies consistently cite it as one of the top contributing rivers globally for plastic emissions.

Field Finding: High concentration of floating multi-layer flexible sachets recorded during wet monsoon runoff months.
Biodiversity Hotspot

Verde Island Passage Incursion

Recognized as the global center of shorefish biodiversity, the Verde Island Passage between Luzon and Mindoro experiences marine debris influxes from coastal settlements and passing shipping lanes.

Ecological Status: Microfibers and degraded fragments detected along coral reef structures and feeding waters.
Benthic Survey

Manila Bay Bottom Sedimentation

Sediment core studies reveal high densities of microplastics buried within Manila Bay's benthic seabed layer. Heavy polymer fractions settle into bottom sludge, affecting bottom-dwelling organisms.

Sediment Metric: High ratio of degraded synthetic paint flakes and PET bottle fragments per kilogram of sediment.
Fisheries Impact

Visayan Sea Fishery Contamination

The Visayan Sea is a major commercial fishing ground. Sampling of commercial catches reveals microplastic ingestion across pelagic and demersal fish species harvested for consumer markets.

Market Risk: Ingestion verified in sardine (*Tamban*) and mackerel populations destined for regional markets.
Coastal Buffers

Mangrove Ecosystem Entrapment

Mangrove forests across Philippine shorelines act as natural barrier traps for floating macro-debris. Trapped plastics smother breathing roots (*pneumatophores*) and break down into micro-particles within fish nursery grounds.

Ecosystem Stress: Accelerated root decay and reduced juvenile fish survival in plastic-choked mangrove reserves.
03. Drivers & Root Factors

Root Causes of Waste Leakage

A study of market structures, geography, municipal collection gaps, and recycling economics in the Philippines.

Economic Model

The Sachet Economy & Retail Dynamics

Socioeconomic factors encourage purchasing fast-moving consumer goods in small, low-cost single-use sachets through neighborhood *sari-sari* stores. While budget-friendly for daily wage earners, this packaging format generates millions of multi-layer flexible sachets daily.

Market Driver: High volume of low-unit-cost packaging without sustainable disposal infrastructure.
Infrastructure Deficit

Municipal Collection & Waste Capacity Gaps

Many island municipalities lack material recovery facilities (MRFs), sanitary landfills, or systematic waste collection networks. Overburdened local systems lead to unmanaged waste disposal near shorelines and riverbanks.

System Bottleneck: Low waste collection coverage in remote island communities and informal urban settlements.
Geography & Hydrology

Archipelagic Geography & Monsoon Runoff

With over 7,600 islands and extensive coastlines, the country's geography creates short hydrological distances from land to ocean. High seasonal rainfall, monsoons, and typhoons flush land-based waste directly into sea channels.

Climatic Factor: Storm surges and heavy rains wash informal dumping sites into river basins.
Market Economics

Low Commercial Value of Soft Plastics

Unlike rigid PET bottles or aluminum cans, thin plastic films and multi-layered sachets have virtually no resale value for waste pickers and junk shops. Consequently, these materials are left uncollected in municipal waste streams.

Economic Failure: Lack of financial incentive for informal waste sector retrieval.
Policy Gaps

Corporate Responsibility & EPR Implementation

Historically, consumer goods manufacturers shifted post-consumer waste management costs onto local governments. Recent Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) legislation aims to mandate brand accountability for recovery targets.

Regulatory Shift: Enforcing compliance across multinational brand owners operating locally.
04. Ecological & Human Impact

Consequences on Marine Life & Health

An evaluation of biological ingestion, food web biomagnification, chemical sorption, and economic impacts on coastal communities.

Biological Hazard

Direct Ingestion & Gastrointestinal Blockage

Sea turtles, cetaceans, planktivorous fish, and seabirds mistake floating plastic particles for natural prey. Ingestion leads to internal blockages, digestive tract perforation, false satiety, and starvation.

Marine Impact: Documented stomach blockages in stranded green sea turtles along coastal island shores.
Ecosystem Transfer

Trophic Transfer & Food Web Biomagnification

Microplastics ingested by lower trophic organisms like zooplankton move upward through marine food chains. Larger predatory fish consume contaminated prey, allowing synthetic polymers to accumulate across trophic levels.

Food Web Impact: Accumulation of micro-particles in commercial game fish species and pelagic predators.
Chemical Hazard

Chemical Sorption & Toxic Additive Leachate

Microplastics act as hydrophobic sponge vectors in marine environments, absorbing heavy metals and persistent organic pollutants (POPs) such as PCBs and pesticides. Once ingested, these toxic compounds leach into organism tissues.

Toxicity Effect: Endocrine disruption, organ inflammation, and reproductive impairment in marine species.
Human Health Pathway

Human Exposure Through Seafood Consumption

As microplastics accumulate in commercial seafood like shellfish, sardines, and squid, micro-particles enter human dietary systems. Research investigates potential cellular stress and inflammatory reactions in human tissues.

Human Pathway: Dietary intake of filter-feeding shellfish harvested from contaminated bays.
Socioeconomic Damage

Economic Damage to Tourism & Coastal Fisheries

Accumulated beach plastic degrades natural coastal scenery, impacting ecotourism revenues. Local fishing communities face damaged nets, fouled propellers, and reduced fish catch quality.

Economic Loss: High cleanup expenditures for coastal municipalities reliant on tourism.
