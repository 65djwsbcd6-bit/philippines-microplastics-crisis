01. Classification & Definitions

Plastic Degradation, Particle Sizes & Polymer Types

Macroplastics (> 25 mm)

Large visible plastic waste including discarded water bottles, carrier bags, food containers, abandoned fishing gear, and heavy packaging. These macro-debris represent the primary precursor to smaller persistent pollutants in coastal waters.

Mesoplastics (5 mm – 25 mm)

Intermediate-sized plastic fragments such as bottle caps, broken handles, plastic straws, and industrial virgin resin pellets (nurdles). Mesoplastics often blanket shoreline high-tide lines across coastal shores.

Secondary Microplastics (< 5 mm)

Tiny plastic fragments created when larger consumer plastics undergo mechanical breakdown, wave abrasion, and solar ultraviolet radiation (photodegradation) over prolonged exposure in water bodies.

Primary Microplastics & Microfibers

Intentionally manufactured microscopic particles including exfoliants in cosmetics, industrial abrasives, and synthetic garment fibers (polyester, nylon) shed during domestic laundry cycles into municipal wastewater.

Nanoplastics (< 1 µm)

Sub-micron particles small enough to form colloidal suspensions in seawater. Due to their minute scale, nanoplastics cross cellular barriers, penetrate organ tissues, and persist in aquatic organisms.

Multi-layer Flexible Packaging (MLP)

Composite materials bonding polyethylene, aluminum foil, and polypropylene. Common in sachets, these multi-material films resist biodegradation and are economically unfeasible to recycle using standard methods.

02. Regional Data & Field Assessment

Current Environmental Status in the Philippines

163 Million

Single-use sachets consumed daily nationwide.

52% Residual

Sachets comprise over half of municipal residual waste.

350,000 Tons

Estimated annual plastic entering marine systems.

Pasig River Conduit

Major Urban River Discharges

Scientific assessments continuously rank the Pasig River among top global ocean plastic contributors. Carrying plastic debris from dense urban centers in Metro Manila, it discharges directly into Manila Bay.

Verde Island Passage

Threats to Coral Biodiversity Hotspots

Recognized as the center of global shorefish biodiversity, the Verde Island Passage shows increasing concentrations of floating microparticles and microfibers, endangering sensitive coral ecosystems.

Manila Bay Benthic Layers

Sediment Accumulation

Sediment core sampling reveals heavy concentrations of dense microplastic fragments embedded in bottom sludge across Manila Bay, affecting benthic organisms and bottom-feeding marine life.

Visayan Sea Fisheries

Microplastic Contamination in Commercial Catch

Biological surveys in key fishing grounds demonstrate plastic ingestion in commercial fish species, including sardines, mackerel, and local shellfish consumed in regional markets.

Mangrove Plastic Trapping

Coastal Buffer Entrapment

Philippine mangrove forests act as natural traps for marine macroplastics. Trapped plastics suffocate pneumatophores (breathing roots) and degrade into microparticles directly within nurseries for juvenile fish.

03. Drivers & Systemic Factors

Root Causes of Plastic Contamination

The Sachet Economy & Retail Models

Socioeconomic factors encourage purchasing fast-moving consumer goods in small, low-cost single-use sachets through neighborhood *sari-sari* stores. While budget-friendly, this market structure generates millions of unrecyclable packaging units daily.

Municipal Waste Infrastructure Gaps

Many island municipalities lack adequate sorting facilities, sanitary landfills, or systematic waste collection networks. Overburdened local systems lead to unmanaged waste disposal near shorelines and rivers.

Monsoon Flooding & Island Topography

High seasonal rainfall, monsoons, and frequent typhoons wash uncollected land-based plastic directly into storm drains, agricultural channels, and river systems, transporting waste into ocean basins.

Low Market Value of Soft Plastics

Unlike rigid PET bottles or aluminum cans, thin plastic films and multi-layered sachets have little to no resale value for waste pickers and junk shops, resulting in low recovery and collection rates.

Corporate Responsibility Challenges

For decades, fast-moving consumer goods (FMCG) corporations shifted waste collection costs onto local municipalities. Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) regulations are currently addressing these legacy gaps.

04. Ecosystem & Health Impact

Ecological, Human & Economic Hazards

Direct Ingestion by Marine Species

Sea turtles, cetaceans, planktivorous fish, and seabirds mistake floating plastic particles for food. Ingestion causes physical blockages in digestive tracts, organ damage, false satiety, and eventual starvation.

Trophic Transfer & Biomagnification

Microplastics ingested by zooplankton move upward through marine food chains. Larger predatory fish consume contaminated prey, allowing synthetic polymers and toxic additives to biomagnify across marine ecosystems.

Sorption of Toxic Pollutants

Microplastics act as sponge-like vectors in seawater, absorbing heavy metals and persistent organic pollutants (POPs) such as PCBs and DDT. Once ingested, these toxins can leach into biological tissues.

Human Seafood Consumption Exposure

As microplastics accumulate in commercial seafood (fish, oysters, mussels), humans consume trace synthetic particles. Research continues to investigate potential metabolic, inflammatory, and endocrine implications.

Economic Damage to Tourism & Fisheries

Plastic pollution degrades coastal environments, affecting ecotourism in island destinations. Local fisheries face increased maintenance costs from damaged gear and compromised catch values.
